CalcLM: a ternary transformer that writes TI-BASIC on a TI-84 Plus CE

By cwklurks. Compiled from the project’s write-ups, plans and run records as of 21 November 2026. The write-ups, plans, run records, hardware-check records and notes this report cites are in the author's private research record; the report is the project's public record.

View: Plain view hides the technical record and keeps the plain-language parts.

Abstract

CalcLM is a small language model that runs entirely on a stock TI-84 Plus CE graphing calculator (an eZ80 CPU at 48 MHz, about 150 KB of user RAM) and writes TI-BASIC programs in the calculator's own token format. Its linear layers use ternary weights ({−1, 0, +1}, about 1.58 bits each) learned with quantization-aware training, and inference is pure integer arithmetic that the calculator reproduces bit for bit. Release v2.2 (2.44M ternary weights) generates 0.82 tokens per second on the device; the wider shape (3.74M ternary weights) passed its first hardware check at 0.67 tokens per second, in candidate v2.3-rc2, and runs in the current release, v2.6. The research question since mid-October has been whether the programs work. Early metrics (does it run without an error) were too easy to satisfy, and a math-shaped metric was satisfied by nonsense: a blind audit found 0 of 150 passes correct. A task suite with typed openings and checked answers became the primary metric, and was hardened after blind audits found loopholes in its judge; a stricter rule set, applied offline to saved runs, changed no verdict. Across preregistered experiments E1 to E19 with multi-seed paired tests, four levers raised task correctness: real math programs in the training data, a wider model, training four times longer, and, at that length, a larger model. Others did not help or made it worse: a lower learning rate, distillation from a larger teacher, and a supervised warm start on practice tasks. Training eight times longer gave no detectable further gain. Verified programs for 217 formulas, mixed in at a low share (E 0.0136), were weakly absorbed with no detected transfer to untaught formulas. Three copies of the same programs (E 0.0382) cost 1.09 points of dev correctness against a control trained in the same boot, again with no detected transfer. Programs for 956 formulas (about 4.8 to 4.9 times as many, counted like for like), one copy of each, showed no transfer either: at the low share a gain of 0.8 tasks on the sparse untaught pool and a dev gain of 0.8 points are both excluded, and at the three-copy share, with about 100 passes per program instead of 340, dev still fell 0.90 points. Whether repetition contributed to the three-copy loss is neither shown nor excluded. Across these three experiments, verified-program breadth data has not shown transfer to unseen formulas at the device model's size. Scoring the shipped decoder on the task suite was inconclusive, confounded by a seeding artifact. The best recipe that fits the calculator, the wide model trained for 60,000 steps, reaches 4.73% dev task correctness (16 seeds), against a milestone of 10%. Model size binds at this scale: the same recipe with 8 layers instead of 6 (W8, 1.32× the weights) scored 6.60% against 5.00% for a control from the same boot (+1.60 points, 16 seeds), the first 16-seed preregistered pass for a model-size change at full training length. W8 does not fit the calculator's memory, and a deeper, narrower 9 × 192 shape of about the wide model's size was not detected. Even W8 is about 3.4 points short of the milestone. The gains so far are mostly reliability on formulas the model already partly knew. Formulas it has never seen are solved about 0.24% of the time by the 60,000-step recipe and 0.71% by W8. E19 re-scored W8 and the 60,000-step control, without training, with 4-bit key/value caches, which target the memory checks W8 fails. W8 still beat the control when both used 4-bit caches (5.87% against 4.41%, +1.46 points, 16 seeds); a shrink of its int8 gain by 0.8 points or more is excluded, and no shrink was detected. Against the shipped int8 format, W8 with 4-bit caches was ahead by 0.87 points, a pass that rests on one task. Post-training 4-bit caches cost W8 0.73 points and the control 0.60, and neither cost is bounded below 0.5. By lab.budget estimates, 4-bit caches let W8 pass the RAM checks but not the AppVar count, file size or cycle budget. E20 then measured W8 with 4-bit caches in CEmu, in a build of the calculator's C core that is bit-exact with the Python reference: 102.3 M cycles per token, 0.469 tokens per second extrapolated to the calculator (1.76× v2.2, above today's 1.5× budget under every reading; rc2 runs at 0.67), 20 model AppVars where 16 are allowed and 1,247,280 bytes of model data against 1.2 MB. Its RAM fits if the C libraries need under 22,755 bytes, which was not measured. E21 fine-tuned W8's 16 checkpoints for 6,000 steps with the 4-bit caches simulated in training (QAT). It scored 5.61% against post-training 4-bit's 5.87% (−0.26 points, 95% CI −0.94 to +0.43), so recovering half or more of the 4-bit cost is excluded; against the shipped int8 control it was +0.61 (p 0.035), which fails Holm and is inconclusive. A matched fine-tune without the simulated 4-bit caches showed no QAT-specific benefit. Under E21's preregistered decision rule (row 6) the device case for a W8-sized model weakens, and nothing is ported before the author revisits the device budget. E22 retrained the 60,000-step recipe on cleaned data (two source types dropped, credit and address patterns scrubbed inside strings, duplicates removed and shared spans capped). Its planned first question, whether credit lines in the output fell, was lost before launch: no local labelling model passed validation, so the credit-pattern count became descriptive (amendment R3). Dev correctness was non-inferior within 0.75 points (+0.66, 95% CI −0.24 to +1.57, against a retrained control that drew low, so no gain is claimed), and copying of real training text was not reduced (0.54% against 0.48% of programs). E23 trained W8 from scratch with the 4-bit caches simulated: its 4-bit fit improved by 43.2% of E19's fit cost (CI 0.5% to 86.0%), but its task score did not follow (−0.52 points, CI −1.25 to +0.21; a gain of half E19's task cost is excluded), so post-training quantization stays the default. E24 trained both shapes fresh on the cleaned data and compared them in their device formats: W8 with 4-bit caches scored 6.17% against 5.25% for the 60,000-step shape at int8 (+0.92 points, CI +0.02 to +1.81, p 0.0225, 16 seeds), a marginal pass that survives dropping its best task; whether the margin reaches one point is neither shown nor excluded, and W8's measured device costs are unchanged. At 6.17%, W8 in device form is about 3.8 points short of the milestone. The author deferred adoption to an exploratory sweep of the on-device topic menu, with its rules stated in advance, over 64 E22 and E24 checkpoints (68,000 programs at device settings): W8c4's arm led L4c's by 3.3 points of menu hit rate (24.7% against 21.4%, CI +0.4 to +6.3), but on fresh seeds the best single checkpoints tied (28.6% for W8c4-s14, 28.4% for E24's L4c-s11, 28.2% for v2.5-rc1's model), and choosing among one arm's checkpoints mattered about as much as the arm. The author then declined the W8 port, adopted the cleaned data for its cleanliness rather than for a measured gain, and chose E24's L4c-s11 for v2.6-rc2. It gets 280 of 1,000 menu programs right (v2.5's model 285, no difference shown; v2.6-rc1 233), and it passed its hardware check on 21 November, byte-identical on the two programs compared. The author released it that day as v2.6, the checked v2.6-rc2 bundle unchanged.

AThe whole story in plain words

This section and the next are for readers with no background in AI or statistics. Each claim restates the technical sections, which hold the full detail. Each experiment in Section 5 also opens with a short plain-language box.

What CalcLM is

CalcLM is a tiny AI that writes programs on a TI-84 Plus CE graphing calculator. It is the same basic kind of system as a chatbot, a language model that writes one small piece of text at a time by predicting what comes next, but far smaller, with one job: writing programs in TI-BASIC, the calculator's own language. There is no internet and no computer attached. The calculator does every step itself, at about one program word (a token, such as Prompt or +) every 1.2 to 1.5 seconds, so a program takes several minutes.

Fitting an AI on a calculator is the hard part: it has about 150 KB of working memory, less than one phone photo. So each of the 2.44 million internal numbers in the model's main layers (its weights) may only be −1, 0 or +1, which turns the arithmetic into adding, subtracting or skipping. Every release is checked on the author's real calculator: it must write exactly the same program, byte for byte, as the reference version on a PC.

What "correct" means

A program counts as correct only if it computes the right answer when run in CEmu, an emulator: a faithful software copy of the calculator on a PC, so thousands of programs can be tested quickly. The project learned the hard way why this matters. Its first scores asked whether a program ran without an error, then whether it looked like a math tool. Both were too easy: in E4 the "math tool" score rose fifteen-fold, yet a careful reader found that none of 150 passing programs used the right formula (one computed a circle's area as πR³).

So the project built a task suite of 40 specific requests, such as the area of a circle or Fahrenheit to Celsius. The model is given the opening of a program (a title and the lines that ask for inputs) and writes the rest. Each attempt runs with three sets of inputs and passes only if it shows the right answer every time. The score, task_correct, is the share of attempts that pass. The checker has since been tightened twice, after reviewers found loopholes.

The goal, and how far away it is

The current milestone, M3, is reached when a recipe that could ship on the calculator gets 10% of attempts right on the 40 tasks, in a properly controlled test. The best recipe that fits the calculator scores 4.73%, about 1 attempt in 21 (later retrainings of it scored 4.45%, 5.00% and 4.57%). A bigger model scored 6.60% but does not fit yet; with the memory-saving notes tested in E19 it scored 5.87%, and a short round of training with those notes built in (E21) scored 5.61%. Trained on cleaned data and run the way the calculator would run it, the bigger model scored 6.17% against 5.25% for today's shape on the same data (E24). So the project is roughly halfway, and even the bigger model is about 3.4 to 4.6 points short.

What was tried, and what was learned

The right examples matter. Real math programs written by people were the best training data found (E4, E6). Math programs written by another AI helped less, and on the older score mostly taught the look of a math tool.

A wider model helped (E7, E7b). W, the widest shape the calculator comfortably holds (about 1.5 times the weights), beat the original by about 0.7 to 1.3 points and became the base model.

Two shortcuts made things worse. Imitating a larger "teacher" model (distillation, E8) made the model a better mimic of text and worse at exact formulas. Drilling it on 212 practice tasks (E9) went like a student cramming one set of practice problems: it learned them, 82% of the programs it wrote on its own began like one of them, and its score fell from 3.71% to 1.20%, with the biggest losses on formulas it used to get right.

Training longer helped (E10), then stopped helping (E11). Four times as much training raised the score from 3.41% to 4.73%, at no cost on the calculator, because the model stays the same size. It worked like more study time with the same textbook: formulas the model half knew became more reliable, but the number of different tasks it solved did not grow. Eight times as much gave no further gain that could be detected (a gain as large as E10's was ruled out, a gain of up to about a point was not), so longer training was set aside.

Teaching more formulas has not worked so far (E13, E15, E18). On 20 held-back tasks whose formulas are never used in training, the model is right about 0.24% of the time, about 1 attempt in 400. So three experiments added breadth data: thousands of extra example programs, written by another AI (GPT-6.1-Sol) and checked in the emulator, covering formulas the model rarely or never solved.

  • E13: 217 formulas, about 1.4% of the training text. The model picked up a little of what it was shown (weak evidence), showed no detectable gain on formulas it was not shown, and its main score did not change.
  • E15: the same programs three times over, to reach the planned 3.8%. The main score fell 1.09 points, lower in 13 of 16 runs, still with no detectable gain on unseen formulas.
  • E18: 956 formulas, nearly five times as many, each program shown once. At the small share there was again no gain on unseen formulas, and a gain of the size the test was built to find was ruled out. At the larger share the main score fell 0.90 points.

A student given stacks of worked examples might be expected to get better at formulas in general. This model did not: extra example programs have not helped it solve formulas it was never shown, and in larger doses they lowered its score on familiar ones. One explanation, not yet tested directly, is that a model this small has limited room, so new material pushes out what it knew. This is a finding about this data at this model size, not proof that teaching more formulas can never work.

A bigger model helped (E16). Giving the model 8 layers instead of 6 (W8, about a third more weights) raised the score from 5.00% to 6.60%, higher in 13 of 16 runs, with matching gains on the held-back tasks: a student with a bigger notebook has more room for formulas. The catch is that W8 does not fit the calculator. The notes it keeps while writing are too big for the memory, it needs 20 data files where 16 are allowed, and it would take about 1.78 times v2.1's time per word, beyond the 1.5 times allowed.

Can the bigger model be made to fit? (E19.) While writing, the model keeps running notes about every word so far, the K/V cache, and looks back over them before each new word. Each number in those notes takes 8 bits today. Storing it in 4 bits halves the space, like a coarser shorthand. E19 checked, without new training, whether W8 keeps its lead with coarser notes, by re-scoring the existing W8 and control models with 4-bit notes under rules frozen before any result existed. The bigger model still wins when both use the memory-saving format: 5.87% against 4.41%, higher in 13 of 16 runs. The format itself costs each model a little, about 0.6 to 0.7 points, before any retraining. Against what ships today (the control with 8-bit notes, 5.00%), W8 with 4-bit notes is ahead by 0.87 points, but that lead rests mostly on one task, Celsius to Fahrenheit. By the project's estimates (not yet measured), the memory problem is solved, but storage and speed are not: W8 still needs 20 data files where 16 are allowed, a larger model file than budgeted, and more time per word than allowed (Section 5.19).

Measuring the bigger model on the emulated calculator (E20). A small test build added 4-bit notes to the calculator's own program and ran W8 in CEmu. It wrote exactly what the reference on the PC writes, number for number. It needs about 102 million steps of work per word, which works out to about 0.47 words per second on the real calculator, if the emulator's timing carries over as it did on two earlier checks. That is about 1.8 times v2.2's work per word, above the 1.5 times allowed, and about 30% slower than v2.3-rc2: the check program would take about 11 minutes instead of 8. The notes now fit in memory, and the rest of the working memory fits if the calculator's standard code libraries are small enough, which was not measured. W8 still needs 20 data files where 16 are allowed, and 1.25 MB of model data where 1.2 MB is allowed (Section 5.20).

Practising with the coarse notes did not help (E21). Switching a finished W8 to 4-bit notes cost it about 0.7 points. E21 tried to win that back: each of the 16 W8 models trained a little longer (6,000 more steps, a tenth of its original training) while practising with 4-bit notes, like a student practising on the calculator they will use in the exam. The practised models scored 5.61% against 5.87% without the practice, and winning back even half of the cost is ruled out. Against what ships today they were ahead by 0.61 points, but that did not clear the stricter two-question pass mark, so the result is inconclusive. A second group got the same extra training without the 4-bit practice, and the practice itself showed no benefit that could be detected. Every version with the extra training scored a little below the model it started from, but none of those dips is established, so this is not evidence that the extra training did harm. By the rule fixed in advance, the case for putting a W8-sized model on the calculator is now weaker, and nothing will be ported until the author revisits the budget calls: the speed limit, whether to pursue a W8-sized model, and the file limits. Training with 4-bit notes from the very start, rather than as a short add-on, came next (E23, below; Section 5.21).

Cleaner training data (E22). A review of the programs the model writes found credit lines naming people, often minors, in about 15% of them, and some copying of training text. E22 cleaned the training data as one package: it dropped two kinds of source, deleted text inside strings where fixed credit, byline, copyright, address and name patterns match, removed duplicate programs and capped every shared block of code. It then retrained today's recipe on it, with the old recipe retrained alongside, 16 runs each. The plan's first question, whether credit lines in the output fell, was lost before launch: no local labelling model could be validated against the author's own labels, so the count of credit patterns became a description rather than a test (amendment R3). Accuracy was not worse beyond the margin set in advance (5.23% against 4.57%; no gain is claimed, because the retrained comparison drew low). Copying of real training text did not fall. The patterns the cleaner removes almost vanished from the output, but that count sees only what the patterns match, so it does not show the model is free of credit lines or names. The author adopted the cleaned data on 11 November, for its cleanliness rather than for a measured gain (Section 5.22; Section 5.25).

Training with 4-bit notes from the start (E23). E23 trained W8 from scratch with the 4-bit rounding simulated in every step, beside ordinary W8 trained again on the same 16 seeds, and scored both at 4-bit. Its fit to held-out text improved, by 43% of E19's fit cost with a wide interval (0.5% to 86%), but its task score did not follow: 5.39% against 5.91%, and a gain of even half of E19's 0.7-point cost is ruled out. So rounding after training stays the default (Section 5.23).

The bigger model in its calculator form, on the cleaned data (E24). Any future release would train on the cleaned data, so E24 trained W8 and today's shape fresh on it, 16 runs each, and scored each the way it would run on the calculator: W8 with 4-bit notes, today's shape with 8-bit notes. W8 scored 6.17% against 5.25%, ahead by 0.92 points, with an interval from almost nothing (0.02) to almost two points (1.81). It stays ahead when its best task is dropped, and the held-out and stricter test suites lean the same way without being significant on their own. Whether the lead reaches a full point, the bar for a slower release, is neither shown nor ruled out, and W8's speed and file problems are unchanged. The author put the adoption call off until the formula menu was measured on the E22 and E24 models (Section 5.24).

Which trained model writes the best menu programs? (the menu sweep). The calculator’s topic menu types the opening of one of ten formula programs, such as cylinder volume, and lets the model finish it. Each of the 64 models from E22 and E24 (four recipes, 16 runs each) wrote 1,000 menu programs, run exactly as the calculator would run them, and the emulator checked every one. This was a measurement with its rules written down in advance, not a test fixed to decide anything. The bigger model in its calculator form got 24.7% right on average against 21.4% for today’s shape: ahead by 3.3 points, with an interval from 0.4 to 6.3. But runs of the same recipe differ from each other by about 3 to 4 points, so picking the best run mattered about as much as picking the recipe. On 1,000 fresh programs each, the best bigger-model run (28.6%), the best run of today’s shape on the cleaned data (28.4%) and the model in v2.5-rc1 (28.2%) were tied, while the run chosen for v2.6-rc1 got 23.2%. So the author declined to port the bigger model: on the menu its best run only ties, and a port would take weeks of device work, run slower on the calculator (about 0.47 against 0.67 words per second) and break the file limits. The author adopted the cleaned data because it is cleaner, not because it was shown to score better (its 1.6-point menu gain is not shown), and chose that tied run of today’s shape on the cleaned data for v2.6-rc2 (Section 5.25).

On the calculator today is release v2.6, released on 21 November (below); the release before it, v2.2, has the original small model. A candidate with the wide model trained four times as long, v2.3-rc2, passed its check on the author's calculator on 28 October, at 0.67 words per second. Two later candidates are built but not yet checked on a calculator: v2.4 adds safety rules (no credit lines in text, no calls to the user's other programs, no settings changes that outlive the program), and v2.5-rc1 adds a menu of formulas to pick from, each shown with its measured hit rate. A candidate on the cleaned data, v2.6-rc1, was not shipped: on the run chosen for it, the menu got fewer formulas right (233 against 285 of 1,000). After the menu sweep, the author chose E24’s L4c-s11 for v2.6-rc2. It is built and gets 280 of 1,000 menu programs right (v2.5: 285, no difference shown; v2.6-rc1: 233). It passed its check on a calculator on 21 November, the first calculator run of the safety rules and the menu: two of its programs came out byte for byte as on the PC (the sheet’s first program was overwritten before it was saved, so it was not compared). The author released it that day as v2.6, the same files, unchanged. A browser page that runs the calculator's own code, and writes exactly the calculator's programs, works on the author's computer; its code is published, but it needs the model files, which are not.

BHow to read the results

The experiment sections use a few statistical ideas over and over. Here they are in plain words, with one worked example from E16 (Section 5.17).

Why 16 copies, compared in pairs

Training an AI involves chance: the model's starting numbers and the order in which it reads its training text are set by a random number called a seed. Two runs of the same recipe with different seeds can score quite differently. An early study found that retraining with another seed, or stopping training slightly later, moved one score by up to 12 points. Comparing one run of recipe A with one run of recipe B would be like ranking two teaching methods by one quiz from one student each.

So every experiment since E10 uses 16 copies of each recipe, trained with seeds 11 to 26, and compares them in pairs: copy 11 of recipe A against copy 11 of recipe B, which use the same seed. Each pair gives one difference, and the 16 differences are averaged. Pairing cancels part of the luck the two runs share. Seed 10 is a rehearsal run that checks everything works; it never counts.

A worked example

E16 asked whether a bigger model (W8) beats the current recipe (L4). W8 scored higher in 13 of the 16 pairs. On average W8 got 6.60% of attempts right and L4 5.00%, a difference of +1.60 percentage points. Three numbers say how far to trust that:

  • p 0.0009. A p value answers one question: if W8 were really no better, how often would luck alone produce a lead at least this large? Here, about 1 time in 1,100. The usual pass mark is p below 0.05, or 1 time in 20. When an experiment asks two main questions, the mark is made stricter (the Holm correction) so that asking more questions does not give luck more chances. A small p says an effect is very likely real; it does not say the effect is large.
  • 95% confidence interval, +0.69 to +2.50. This is the range of true effect sizes that fit the data. W8's real gain could plausibly be as small as 0.7 points or as large as 2.5. Because the whole range is above zero, a gain is shown.
  • One-sided upper bound. Some tables also give the largest effect the data still allow, at the same 95% level. It decides whether an effect is "ruled out", below.

"Not detected" is not "ruled out"

When a test fails, the paper says the effect was not detected: the data cannot tell it apart from zero. That does not mean the change does nothing. E11 is the example. Training eight times as long gave +0.27 points, with an interval from −0.51 to +1.05. A gain of about one point is still inside that range, so it was not detected, but not excluded either.

An effect is ruled out (the paper also says "excluded") only when the upper bound falls below a size fixed in advance. The recent plans use +0.8, which is about the gain E16's plan predicted for a bigger model and about the size the breadth tests were built to catch. In E18, the low-share breadth arm's gain on formulas it was never shown had an upper bound of +0.48 tasks, and its main-score gain an upper bound of +0.22 points. Both are below +0.8, so gains of that size are ruled out. That is a stronger statement than "not detected".

Guards: tripwires

A guard is a tripwire that fails the experiment if something else got worse, even when the main question looks fine. Guards check, for example, that the calculator's whole-number arithmetic still agrees with the model, that programs still run cleanly, and that the model is not copying its training programs. In the breadth experiments, guard a required the main score not to fall by more than 0.5 points. In E15 it fell by 1.09, so guard a failed, and the verdict reads "interference".

Preregistration: no moving the goalposts

Before any training, each experiment's plan states the question, the score that decides it, the guards, the statistical test and the pass mark. An independent reviewer (a separate AI model) checks the plan and the code. Experiments were run by AI coding assistants under the author’s direction; the author approved plans and budgets. Then everything is frozen: a fingerprint of the code, plan and data is stored, and the software refuses to produce the deciding numbers if anything has changed. Any later change is recorded openly as an amendment, together with what had been seen at the time. Because the rules exist before the results, no one can pick whichever measure happens to look best afterwards. This matters in practice: three times, a choice that looked good on a single rehearsal run did not hold up on fresh runs (E5, E7b, E8).

Blind audits

The checker only looks at the numbers on the screen, so a program can pass by accident, for example by using a spare variable that happens to hold a useful value. So for each recipe a fresh reviewer model reads 50 passing programs without knowing which recipe wrote them, and labels each one genuine (it computes the task's formula), partial (right answer, visible flaw) or coincidental (right number for the wrong reason). Not knowing the recipe keeps the reviewer impartial. Audits found most of the checker's loopholes, and they show that between 70% and 100% of passes are genuine, depending on the recipe and the reviewer. From E22 on, the reviewer was a local open-weight model that had not been validated against human labels, with labelling instructions written after the results were known, so those later audits are weaker (Section 7.3).

Share, or dose

In the breadth experiments, the share (written E, and also called the dose) is the fraction of all training text that is breadth programs. E 0.0136 means about 1.4 parts in 100; E 0.0382 means about 3.8. A related number, passes per program, counts how many times training reads each breadth program: about 121 in E13, 340 in E15 (three copies) and 100 in E18's larger arm.

Which tasks are counted

Dev tasks are the 40 main tasks, the ones M3 is measured on. Held-out tasks are 20 more whose formulas are never used for training, so they test new formulas. The breadth experiments add practice pools: taught tasks, whose formulas appear in the breadth data, and untaught tasks, whose formulas were deliberately left out to see whether learning spreads to them. T_sparse counts, per training run, how many of 71 untaught tasks that the control model almost never solves get solved at least once.

1Introduction

The project's north star, in the project's vision document, is a calculator that writes its own programs with every number checkable. A student types the opening of a program, for example a title and Prompt A,B,C, or picks a topic. A few minutes later a new program, prgmGEN, exists on the calculator, asks for values and shows a result that depends on them. The demonstration is to be shown with an honest hit rate.

Why a calculator. The founding research (12 October) searched about 59 queries in six languages and found no transformer language model running natively on any graphing calculator, TI or otherwise; the closest was a handwritten-digit CNN on the same calculator. Ternary, multiply-free transformers on 8-bit-class hardware are prior art (retro game consoles and a BBC Micro in 2026), and so is packing five ternary weights per byte for table lookups. What is new is the target, a graphing calculator, and the task: the model emits the calculator's own program bytecode. The constraint shapes everything. The model must fit in about 700 KB of archive flash and about 107 KB of free RAM, with no host computer at generation time.

Why math first. Games were the first idea, but only 8.7% of human programs in the validation set are interactive and 1.7% are real games. Input-driven math programs are common in the corpus and can be checked automatically. So math tools became the target and games the stretch goal.

2System

Figure 2 shows the path from scraped programs to a program written on the calculator. Each stage is described below.

2.1Data and tokens

The corpus holds TI-BASIC programs for the TI-83 and TI-84 family from ticalc.org (including a 2014 Internet Archive crawl), TI-Planet and public GitHub repositories. Cemetech was left out because its robots.txt opts out of AI-training collection. After exact deduplication and filtering there are 55,835,585 training tokens in 41,389 programs, and 666 validation programs. 67.2% of programs are longer than the model's 256-token context (median 512 tokens), so the model mostly trains on program middles. The corpus is for local research only and is never redistributed.

The tokenizer needs no learning. A TI-BASIC program is stored on the calculator as a sequence of one- and two-byte tokens, and those tokens are the vocabulary: 781 ids, including 4 specials (padding, begin, end, unknown). Generating a program means emitting the bytes the calculator itself stores.

Later experiments add math-focused data. SEED adds 2,413 real human programs that pass the math tier, mined from the training split. SYN adds 3,000 synthetic programs written by an outside LLM (GPT-6.1-Sol), each verified in the emulator. From E7 on, SEED's mix (58,281,008 token ids, 46,698 programs) is the standard training set.

2.2Model

The model is a llama2.c-style decoder: RMSNorm, SwiGLU feed-forward layers, and multi-query attention with one shared key/value head of width 32, which keeps the key/value cache small enough for calculator RAM. Version 1 used learned positions; from v2 on, positions are ALiBi (a fixed per-head distance penalty), which removed a 48.6 KB position table and lets generation run past the 256-token window with a sliding ring cache, up to 1,024 tokens.

W is the widest shape the device allows. Depth is capped by RAM: the key cache lives in a 60,690-byte region, and a seventh layer would need 66,041 bytes. Width is capped by the program's 16-AppVar limit and the kernel's 635-input limit. W sits within 4% of the largest legal shape and was chosen for being cheaper on the device.

2.3Ternary weights and quantization-aware training

Every linear layer stores weights in {−1, 0, +1} with one scale per output row, in the style of BitNet b1.58. Inputs to each linear layer are int8 values with one scale per vector. Training keeps full-precision latent weights and quantizes them in the forward pass (quantization-aware training, QAT), so the network learns to work with the coarse weights it will actually have. Each run averages the latent weights over its last 3,000 steps; G1 found this alone lowered validation loss from 1.2269 to 1.1989 on the v1 recipe. A run of the W shape takes about 6 minutes on the project's GPU at 15,000 steps, and about 23 minutes at 60,000.

2.4Integer reference and export

model/intref.py is the normative definition of inference. It uses integers only: rounding shifts, small pseudo-float scales, and exported lookup tables for softmax and SiLU. The calculator must reproduce its logits bit for bit and its sampled tokens exactly for a given seed. On v2's export, intref and the QAT model agree on the next token 98.95% of the time and differ in validation loss by 0.001%. The exporter packs five ternary weights into each byte (35 = 243 ≤ 256) and splits the model into AppVars of at most 65,505 bytes, stored in archive flash and read in place.

2.5The on-device engine and its kernels

Because one byte encodes five weights, it can index a 243-entry table of precomputed partial sums for each group of five inputs. One lookup and one add replace five multiply-accumulates. The M2 kernel, in eZ80 assembly, runs at 7.3 to 7.5 cycles per weight when its code executes from flash (10.6 to 11.0 from RAM; the C reference takes 65 to 68). It is bit-identical to the Python reference in all 100 tests. Attention uses unsigned 8×8 multiplies on biased caches. Moving each hot loop to assembly and finally running the routines from flash took a token at position 0 from 230 M cycles to 44 M (Figure 3).

Memory is tight. The key cache, a 2 KB token buffer and the core state fill 60,326 of 60,690 bytes of OS scratch RAM. The value cache is a temporary 48 KB AppVar in user RAM. The kernel's lookup tables and the activations borrow half of video RAM while the screen runs in 8-bit mode. If free RAM is short, the program shrinks its context window rather than failing.

2.6Decoding

Three decoder features sit between the weights and the output, all reproduced on the calculator:

  • Logit masks from a TI-BASIC tracker that follows the program as it is written and bans tokens that must error, such as reading a string never assigned. They cost about 0.245 M cycles per token (0.4%).
  • ALiBi horizons: heads whose distance penalty has saturated skip far keys. This cut cycles per token by 10.8% at 1,024 tokens (E1).
  • LD2, the linker decoder: the tracker acts as an incremental linker. Labels must pay off pending jumps, the program cannot end while jumps are unresolved, and a fitted stop hazard ends programs at plausible points (E2b).

Sampling is temperature 0.8 with top-k 20, using an xorshift random generator that the calculator matches exactly.

3Measuring whether a program works

Most of the project's effort since 14 October has gone into this question, and the answer changed four times. Each change came from finding that the previous metric could be satisfied by programs that do not work.

3.1Unprompted run metrics

The first yardstick took 1,000 unprompted samples per model and ran each on emulated TI-OS 5.8 (CEmu, with the author's own ROM, clean state per program). It recorded whether the program finished without an ERR: screen, and strict OK: it ended normally and showed something beyond echoing its inputs. Later work added computed2 (a visible value that is not a string literal from the program and not only 0 or ±1), the combined error rate LABEL + UNDEFINED + SYNTAX, and complete (ended with an end token within 1,024 tokens). By these measures v1 already looked human: 58.8% of its programs ran without error against 51.1% of truncated human programs. The v1 write-up noted the bar was low. Samples read as plausible but were often incoherent, for example a Goto with no matching label.

A noise study on 13 October set the statistical approach used ever since. Sampling noise was small, but retraining the same recipe with another seed, or taking a checkpoint 250 steps later, moved the no-error rate by up to 12 points. Model-to-model noise dominates, so every comparison needs several training seeds, compared as means.

3.2The math tier, and why it failed

The math tier (M2) runs a program twice, with inputs 2 and 7, and passes it if it takes input, computes, answers and responds to the input. It works on human programs: 25 of 32 human validation passes were usable math tools. E4 trained on 3,000 verified synthetic math programs and raised the math-tier rate from 1.79% to 26.25% (Figure 4). Its preregistered blind audit then labelled 50 passes per arm without knowing the arm. None of the 150 was correct; 130 were trivial (garbled menus, arbitrary arithmetic under nonsense labels, decorated echoes). The synthetic data had taught the look of a math tool, with a title, prompts and a computed answer, and the wrong formula, such as πR³ for a circle's area.

3.3Task suite v1: asking for a specific tool

The task suite (18 October) changes the question from "write some math" to "write this math". Each of 40 tasks, five per topic across geometry, physics, algebra, finance, conversions, chemistry, number theory and statistics, gives the model a typed opening (a title and the prompts). The model completes it 25 times per checkpoint. Each completion runs in CEmu with three input tuples, never 2 or 7. A sample passes if every run shows the reference answer to within 0.1%, shows no other tuple's answer, and ends without error. task_correct is passes over samples. All 40 hand-written gold completions pass and all 115 wrong mutants fail. On E4's checkpoints the suite ranked the arms the opposite way from the math tier: real math programs (2.50%) beat synthetic ones (1.88%), which beat no math data (0.62%).

3.4Loopholes found by audits

From E6 onward every experiment ran a blind audit: a fresh reviewer model labelled 50 passes per arm as genuine (computes the task's formula), partial (right answer with a user-visible defect) or coincidental (right number for the wrong reason), seeing only the task and the program text. The audits found these holes in the harness:

  • Extra prompts (E6, E7): a prompt beyond the task's variables received the tuple's last value again, so a formula using a spare variable could still hit the answer. All 3 coincidental passes in E7 came from this.
  • Unset variables (E6): an unset variable is 0 in a fresh emulator, while a calculator in use holds old values.
  • Number spray (literature review before E9): a program that prints several candidate numbers can pass, because the judge only forbade other tuples' answers. E7b's audit saw spray, always with a correct value among the numbers. E9's training reward requires the reference to be the only or last number shown.
  • The same decoy everywhere (E8): suite v2 answered every extra prompt with the same decoy, so a circle-area program computing πR²·H/B got H/B = 1 and passed. Distinct decoys per prompt close it.
  • A reverse loophole (E9): pre-filling variables made 2 partial passes depend on a variable being nonzero. It is noted for a later suite version.

3.5Task suite v2 and held-out tasks

Suite v2 (20 October) answers extra prompts with a decoy and pre-fills A–Z and θ with distinct random nonzero values before each run. It adds 20 held-out tasks in statistics, number theory, chemistry, finance, algebra and physics that are never used for training data, synthetic prompts or rewards, and a labelled variant that also requires a fitting label word next to the number. All 60 golds pass, 175 mutants fail, 20 loophole mutants that passed under v1 now fail, and none of the 9 audited coincidental passes survives. Re-scoring old checkpoints lowered task_correct by only 1 to 6% relative (Figure 5), so earlier conclusions stood. Two other findings mattered more. Only about half of correct numbers carry a fitting label. And on the held-out tasks every arm scored 0 to 0.2%: 23 passes in 20,000 samples.

3.6Task suite v3: stricter rules, applied offline

Suite v3 (28 October) answers two harness points the audits kept raising. A sample passes v3 only if it passes v2 and, on every tuple, two more rules hold. No TIMEOUT: the run must end normally; v2 accepted a timeout, so a program that never ends could pass. The reference is the last number shown, within v2's tolerance; this blocks number spray and contradictory later answers, as E9's training reward does. v3 changes no file a frozen experiment hashes, and it runs offline. It rebuilds each sample's records from the saved driver records, and v2 recomputed from them equals the saved judgments row for row, in every field, in all 235 saved directories (E10, E11, E13, E14 and E8's W held-out), so the offline reproduction of v2 is exact. All 60 golds pass v3, with the reference last on all 180 gold tuples and no timeouts. All 175 mutants and the 20 v2 loophole mutants still fail, and 5 new hand-written loophole mutants (2 that never end, 2 that show a second formula after the answer, 1 that shows a stale prefilled variable) pass v2 and fail v3. M3 stays on the v2 metric for now (the author, 28 October); v3 is reported as a secondary.

4Method: preregistration

Every roadmap experiment follows the same discipline (the author, 19 October). Its purpose is to make a result hard to talk into existence after the fact.

  1. Plan. A written plan states the question, arms, primary metric, guards, statistical test, power and every definition before any training. It includes a selection history: which results were already seen when the thresholds were set.
  2. Independent review. A separate reviewer model reviews the plan and the runner code before the freeze. Critical, high and medium findings are fixed first.
  3. Development on seed 10. One development seed runs end to end before the freeze. It may choose a parameter by a rule fixed in advance (E7's learning rate, E8's teacher and mixing weight), and it checks determinism: re-sampling must reproduce earlier samples byte for byte.
  4. Freeze. A snapshot hashes the code, the plan, the data and every reused artifact. The runner refuses to produce decision numbers if a hash changes.
  5. Decision seeds. Fresh training seeds decide, paired by seed across arms. The test is a one-sided paired t-test on the per-seed differences. With two primary tests, Holm's correction splits the 0.05 level.
  6. Guards. Side conditions an arm must not break to be adopted: agreement with the integer reference, validation loss, strict OK, copying of training text.
  7. Amendments in the open. Any change after the plan is committed is recorded as an amendment, with what had been seen at the time.
  8. Write-up. The write-up reports the verdict as the rule gives it, then the secondaries and a blind audit, then caveats. Failures are written as plainly as passes.

The amendments show the process working. E6's scoring crashed on one checkpoint when a program printed a power of ten past float range; the fix was shown to leave all 51 earlier judgments identical, and the author chose it over a full rerun. E9's first warm-start recipe failed its own manipulation check on seed 10 (a 1.7% training-task rate against a floor of 10%), and a recorded seed-10 sweep chose a stronger recipe while loosening one guard from +0.010 to +0.025 validation loss, before the freeze. E5's frozen runner had a bug in its development table, so the decision was computed with the frozen code rather than a fixed runner.

Seed counts grew as effects shrank. G1 used 3 seeds, E3 to E5 used 5, E1/E2 and E2b used 9 checkpoints, E6 to E7b used 8, and E8 and E9 used 12. E7 failed at p 0.084 partly because the wide model's seed-to-seed spread was twice the small model's. On 22 October, after E9, the author set the 16-seed rule: decision seeds 11 to 26 for E10 and later. In E10's power simulation, 16 seeds give about 69% power for a +0.75-point effect and 94% for +1.0 (per-arm SD 0.81).

Development-seed choices proved optimistic three times. evo's overnight search reported a validation-loss gain of 0.019 on one seed; across fresh seeds it was 0.004 and not significant (E5). The small model's apparent preference for a lower learning rate on seed 10 did not replicate (E7b). E8's chosen teacher improved validation loss by 0.013 on seed 10 and by 0.005 across decision seeds. This is the reason single-seed choices never decide anything.

5.10E8: distillation

21 Oct Fail

In plain terms: Distillation trains the model to copy a larger "teacher" model's guesses instead of learning only from the programs themselves. It lowered correctness (2.98% against 3.53%, lower in 7 of 12 runs) while making the model a slightly better predictor of text, at twice the computer time. A plausible reason is that the teacher's hedged guesses taught hedging, where a correct formula needs one sharp choice.

Decision: Drop distillation and try learning from practice tasks checked in the emulator (E9).

Question
Does training W to match a larger full-precision teacher's predicted distribution improve correctness?
Design
Two teachers (14.0M and 24.4M parameters) had to pass a validation-loss gate. Seed 10 chose T-M with mixing weight α 0.5 (half teacher, half data). W vs distilled WD, 12 seeds.
Result
WD 2.98% vs W 3.53% (−0.56, p 0.95 for better), lower on 7 of 12 seeds (Figure 9). WD had slightly lower validation loss and more clean-running programs, at 2.1× the compute. Both teachers had partly memorized the data: their best checkpoints came about halfway through training, with training loss well below validation loss.
Meaning
Soft targets made the student better at imitating the corpus and worse at exact formulas. A plausible mechanism is hedging where a correct formula needs one sharp choice. The literature review had warned that a null result was likely at this scale.

Question

5.12E10: training longer

23–26 Oct Pass (L4)

In plain terms: Does training the same model longer help? Four times as long raised the score from 3.41% to 4.73% (+1.32 points); luck alone would produce that about once in 30,000 tries (p 3.1e-5). Twice as long did nothing measurable. The model's size does not change, so the gain costs nothing on the calculator. It made formulas the model half knew more reliable; it did not add new ones.

Decision: 60,000 training steps (called L4) became the standard recipe. Its best run, at 6.45%, became the calculator candidate v2.3-rc2; the average, 4.73%, is the honest estimate, because the best of 16 flatters.

Question
W's validation loss was still falling at step 15,000, and training is cheap. Does training W from scratch for 2× or 4× the steps raise task correctness?
Design
W (15,000 steps), L2 (30,000) and L4 (60,000), identical except --steps, sharing initial weights and the first 15,000 batches; 16 seeds; Holm across the two tests; dev k 50 and held-out k 100. W-s11 to W-s22 were reused from E7 and E8 after a reproduction check of W-s10; W-s23 to W-s26 were trained new. The run paused from 24 to 25 October, with the PC powered off at the author’s request, and resumed from its stamps.
Result

Meaning
The first correctness gain since data and shape, at no device cost. The dose-response is late and steep: 2× did nothing measurable. The gain is reliability on formulas the model already partly knew. L4 is the new recipe base, and its best seed, L4-s23 (6.45%, optimistically selected), is v2.3-rc2.

5.13E11: training longer still

26–28 Oct Fail

In plain terms: Does training eight times as long beat four times? It scored 4.72% against 4.45% (+0.27, p 0.24): no further gain was detected, and two of the 16 runs carried most of the difference. A gain as large as E10's is ruled out, but a gain of up to about a point is not. A computer reboot between the two sets of runs also muddies the comparison.

Decision: Longer training was deprioritised, since it costs twice the computer time for at most about a point. L4 stayed the recipe.

Question
E10's gain from length was late and steep, and validation loss was flattening. Does training W for 120,000 steps (8×, L8) beat 60,000 (L4)?
Design
L4 against L8, identical except --steps; 16 seeds paired; dev k 50, held-out k 50 (descriptive). 5.17E16: the capacity probe

5.19E19: the bigger model with 4-bit notes

In plain terms: E16's bigger model (W8) scored better, but the notes it keeps about every word already written (the K/V cache) are too big for the calculator's memory at today's 8 bits per number. The bigger model still wins when both use the memory-saving format: 5.87% against 4.41%, higher in 13 of 16 runs. The format itself costs each model a little, about 0.6 to 0.7 points, before any retraining. Against what ships today (L4 with 8-bit notes, 5.00%), W8 with 4-bit notes is ahead by 0.87 points, but that lead rests mostly on one task, Celsius to Fahrenheit. By the project's estimates, which are not yet measured, the memory problem is solved, but storage and speed are not: W8 still needs 20 files where 16 are allowed, a model file over budget, and more time per word than allowed.

Decision: Measure W8's real speed and memory with 4-bit notes in a small test build on the emulated calculator (CEmu), then try training with 4-bit notes built in (QAT) before any full port to the calculator. Question

Design
An engineering measurement, not a statistical test: success is bit-exactness plus a table of measured numbers, with no thresholds and no seeds. 5.21E21: training with 4-bit notes built in

2–3 Nov Row 6: half recovery excluded; P2 inconclusive

In plain terms: Switching a finished W8 to 4-bit notes cost it about 0.7 points (E19). E21 tried to win that back with practice: each of the 16 W8 models trained 6,000 more steps, a tenth of its original training, with 4-bit notes simulated during training (QAT), and was then scored exactly as E19 scored W8. It did not help. The practised models scored 5.61% against 5.87% for the unpractised ones (−0.26 points, higher in only 7 of 16 runs), and winning back even half of the cost is ruled out. Against what ships today (L4 with 8-bit notes, 5.00%) they were ahead by 0.61 points, but that did not clear the stricter pass mark for two questions (Holm), so the result is inconclusive. A second group got the same extra training without the 4-bit practice, and the practice itself showed no benefit that could be detected. Every version with the extra training scored a little below the model it started from, but none of those dips is established, so this is not evidence that the extra training did harm. Two bug-check thresholds were changed after the freeze, both recorded openly, and one of them corrected a threshold that had been set wrongly; neither touched the training or any score.

Decision: Row 6 of the rule fixed in advance: the case for a W8-sized model on the calculator weakens, and nothing is ported before the author revisits the budget calls O1 to O3. Question

Design

Separately, a local browser demo compiles the device C core to WebAssembly and reproduces 132 of 132 grid programs and the three check-sheet programs byte for byte; its code is published, its model files and outputs are not.

W is close to every hard limit (Figure 13). The fixed RAM regions are at 99% or more, the activation area rises from 86% to 92%, and the model data grows from 677 KB to 997 KB, which needs 318 KB more free archive than v2.2. Within the vision document’s budget (1.5× cycles, 1.2 MB of model files) a deeper model is ruled out by RAM, so the roadmap's next device item is efficiency: 4-bit key/value caches to fit 7 or 8 layers, or sparse kernels that skip zero weights. E16 raised this item's priority: W with 8 layers beat L4 by 1.60 points but fails lab.budget's key cache, value cache and AppVar checks, and exceeds the cycle and file-size limits (Section 5.17, Table 6). E19 found that W8's gain holds with 4-bit key/value caches, which by lab.budget's estimate clear the RAM checks but not the AppVar, file-size or cycle limits (Section 5.19, Table 9). E20 then measured W8 with 4-bit caches in CEmu, in a build of the C core bit-exact with model/kv4.py (Section 5.20, Tables 10 and 11). It needs 102.3 M cycles per token with horizons and LD2, 0.469 tok/s extrapolated to the calculator at r = 1 (not timed on hardware): 1.76× v2.2, about 30% slower than rc2, and outside 1.5× under every reading. BSS (45,834 B) and CLMTMP (61,276 B) fit; user RAM fits if the C libraries need under 22,755 B, which was not measured; and it needs 20 model AppVars against 16 and 1,247,280 B of model data against 1.2 MB. E21's 4-bit QAT fine-tune did not improve W8 at 4-bit, and its decision row 6 applies: the device case for a W8-sized model weakens, and there is no port before the author revisits O1 to O3 (Section 5.21). E23’s from-scratch QAT improved W8’s 4-bit fit but not its task score, so PTQ stays (Section 5.23). E24 retrained both shapes on the cleaned data and compared them in device format: W8 at 4-bit beat L4c at int8 by 0.92 points (CI +0.02 to +1.81), a marginal pass (Section 5.24). E24 changes nothing about W8’s device cost. After the topic-menu sweep (Section 5.25), the author declined the W8 port on 11 November and shelved it for possible later work (Section 8).

7Discussion

7.1What moved correctness and what did not

Figure 14 puts every task-correctness comparison up to E21 (3 November) on one axis; E22 to E24 are in Table 4. Four levers helped. Real human math programs in the training data roughly tripled correctness over no math data (E4, E6). The wider shape helped by about 0.7 to 1.3 points (E7, E7b). Training four times longer helped by 1.32 points (E10). At that length, a larger model helped by 1.60 points (E16: W with 8 layers, 1.32× the weights, against L4), the first 16-seed preregistered pass for a model-size change at full training length; E7b's earlier 8-seed pass was at one lr that favoured W. So model size binds at this scale. E19 re-scored the same W8 and L4 checkpoints, without training, with 4-bit K/V: W8 still led by 1.46 points (p 0.002), a shrink of its int8 gain by 0.8 points or more is excluded, and no shrink was detected; post-training 4-bit cost each model 0.6 to 0.7 points, a cost not bounded below 0.5. E21 then fine-tuned W8 for 6,000 steps with 4-bit K/V simulated in training (QAT), and it did not recover that cost: against post-training 4-bit it was −0.26 (CI −0.94 to +0.43), so half recovery is excluded, and its lead over the shipped L4 (+0.61, p 0.035) failed Holm and is inconclusive; a matched fine-tune without QAT showed no QAT-specific benefit, and the fine-tuned arms' lean below their sources is exploratory, with no two-sided interval excluding zero. A deeper, narrower shape of about W's size (9 × 192) was not detected (−0.30, CI −1.20 to +0.60), which does not exclude it at a better lr. Data and length cost nothing on the device; width costs 25% in speed; W8 does not fit the device with int8 K/V; with 4-bit K/V, measured in CEmu (E20), its RAM fits if the C libraries are small enough, but it costs 1.76× v2.2's cycles by extrapolation and exceeds the AppVar and model-data limits. Training eight times longer gave no detectable further gain (+0.27, p 0.24, E11): a gain as large as E10's is excluded, one of up to about a point is not. Breadth data has not helped so far: verified programs for more formulas have not shown transfer to unseen formulas at W's size in three experiments (E13, E15, E18), and at E15's share they cost dev even with many more formulas and about 100 passes per program (E18, −0.90). That left E16's capacity result as the lever to pursue. It survives 4-bit K/V (E19), but its device price is measured above today's limits (E20), and the QAT fine-tune did not lower its 4-bit cost (E21), so under E21's row 6 the device case for a W8-sized model weakens until the author revisits the budget. E23 then trained W8 with 4-bit K/V from scratch: the 4-bit fit improved (43.2% of E19’s NLL cost, CI 0.5% to 86.0%) but dev did not follow (−0.52, CI −1.25 to +0.21; a gain of half E19’s task cost excluded), so neither QAT route tried has shown a task gain and PTQ stays. E24 retrained both shapes on the cleaned data (SEEDc, E22) and compared them in device format: W8 at 4-bit beat L4c at int8 by +0.92 (CI +0.02 to +1.81, p 0.0225), a marginal pass not shown to rest on one task, with W8’s int8 gain (+1.63) and 4-bit cost (−0.71) close to E16’s and E19’s. So the bigger model still leads after the data change (P1; its int8 gain, +1.63, is descriptive in E24); whether the device-format margin reaches a point is open. E22’s cleaned data itself was non-inferior for L4 on dev (+0.66, CI −0.24 to +1.57) and did not reduce copying. On the topic menu (Section 5.25, exploratory), W8c4’s arm led L4c’s by 3.3 points, but the best single checkpoints tied on fresh seeds, and checkpoints of one arm differed by about as much as the arms did, so on that metric checkpoint selection is a lever about as large as W8.

The levers that failed are as informative. A lower learning rate hurt the small shape. Distillation and the task warm start both made the model a better imitator by some measure (lower validation loss, higher strict OK, a 28% practice-task rate) while lowering correctness on the dev tasks. Validation loss and run-based metrics have repeatedly moved in the opposite direction from correctness, which is why neither is a primary metric any more. E13 adds a case where a run metric moved without correctness: breadth data raised strict OK by 5.1 points in every seed and left dev task correctness unchanged (−0.01). E15 adds a sharper one: three copies of the same data raised strict OK by 10.4 points in every seed while dev task correctness fell 1.09 points. E18 repeats the pattern at both shares: strict OK rose 3.0 and 8.7 points while dev fell 0.36 and 0.90. E16's D9 is a smaller case (strict OK +3.5, uncorrected, with dev not detected), while W8 is a case where validation loss and correctness moved together (−0.024 and +1.60). E23 adds a case where a fit measure moved without correctness: W8q’s 4-bit val NLL improved (p 0.024) while its dev score was −0.52 (not significant), below the +0.32 the fit predicted. Synthetic data on its own changed which formulas the model knew more than how many.

The decoder work (horizons, masks, LD2) improved how often programs run, finish and avoid errors, and made generation faster. E14 scored LD2 on the task suite for the first time, with prompts, on E10's L4 checkpoints. Its result (−0.35, two-sided p 0.093) is inconclusive and confounded by a first-draw seeding artifact in the prompted protocol, so LD2's effect on correctness is still unknown, and the task suite still decodes with v2.1 settings.

7.2Reliability against breadth

The clearest pattern across E4 to E10 is that the model writes formulas it has seen many times and rarely composes new ones. Cylinder volume and circle area make up 63 of 100 audited passes in E7 and 92 of 150 in E7b. On the 20 held-out tasks E10's L4 is right 0.244% of the time; in E16, the reused L4 is right 0.288% and W8, which does not fit the device, 0.713%. The E9 pilot found 8 of 212 practice tasks solved even once in 128 tries. E10's gain made known formulas more reliable (Fahrenheit to Celsius +102 passes, cylinder volume +96), while the number of distinct dev tasks solved across all seeds stayed at 24 to 25. E11's 8× arm moved passes among known formulas (Celsius to Fahrenheit +35, cylinder volume −47; no single shift stands out from seed noise), and its few newly solved tasks rest on one to three passes each. E16's W8 mostly follows the pattern: its largest pooled gains are on known formulas (Fahrenheit to Celsius +111, Celsius to Fahrenheit +92, hypotenuse +67), pooled dev tasks solved rose only from 25 to 27, and the untaught breadth pools were not detected, though its held-out passes more than doubled (114 against 46; uncorrected). E19's 4-bit re-score keeps the pattern: W8's P2 lead over the shipped L4 rests on one known formula, Celsius to Fahrenheit, and 58 of the 100 audited 4-bit passes are cylinder, circle or Fahrenheit to Celsius, though under 4-bit K/V W8 had 84 held-out pooled passes against L4's 19 (uncorrected). E21's QAT fine-tune moved passes among the same known formulas: against W8k4, Celsius to Fahrenheit lost 63 pooled passes and circle area 38, while cylinder volume gained 24 (uncorrected), and Celsius to Fahrenheit's net against L4 fell from +85 in E19 to +22. E24’s gain on the cleaned data is not shown to rest on one task but is still concentrated: dropping its best task (hypotenuse, 28% of the net pooled gain) leaves +0.68 (p 0.041), though two nearly tied tasks carry 56% of it, and held-out moved only +0.11 (CI −0.09 to +0.31). On the topic menu (Section 5.25; per topic, descriptive), W8c4’s lead over L4c sat on Fahrenheit to Celsius (+11.7 points), hypotenuse (+10.1) and Celsius to Fahrenheit (+6.8), formulas both arms already solve, while cylinder volume was lower (−8.1).

E13 tested breadth directly. Verified programs for 217 formulas raised passes on the 82 taught formulas from 2 to 8 in 10,496 samples, weak evidence of absorption, and passes on the 74 untaught formulas from 23 to 24, no detected transfer. That run reached about a third of its planned share (E 0.0136 against 0.03 to 0.04) with about 220 of a planned 400 to 600 formulas. It shows that this low share did not move M3, not that breadth data cannot. E15 then reached the planned share with three copies of the same programs (E 0.0382). Within its boot, dev correctness fell 1.09 points (CI −1.78 to −0.41), transfer to formulas L4 does not already pass was not detected (−0.06 tasks, CI −0.37 to +0.24), and taught-pool passes rose from 5 to 20. The trade is not like for like: dev lost 350 pooled passes, while taught passes rose by 15 and untaught by 9.

E18 then raised the number of formulas instead of the copies: 956 formulas, about 4.8 to 4.9× E13's count like for like, one copy of each program, with the variants of every flagged specification dropped. At E13's share, sparse untaught transfer was exactly flat (0.00 tasks, CI −0.58 to +0.58) and dev moved −0.36 (CI −1.05 to +0.34); gains of +0.8 on either are excluded. At E15's share, with about 100 passes per program against BR3's 340, dev still fell 0.90 points (CI −1.57 to −0.22), sparse transfer was inconclusive (+0.31, upper bound +0.81), and taught-pool tasks with a pass fell to 0. As in E15, the losses sat on L4's most-passed formulas (pooled: hypotenuse −77, cylinder volume −71, circle area −67).

Breadth has now been tested in three experiments, and none helps M3: verified-program breadth data has not shown transfer to unseen formulas at W's size. That is a statement about these data at this size, not a finding that breadth cannot work. Two caveats limit what the experiments say together. First, why dev drops is not settled. E15's copies raised share and repetition at once (about 340 passes per program against 121); E18's BR-wide-hi held the share, cut repetition to about a third, and still lost dev. Its contrast with BR3 (+0.20, CI −0.53 to +0.93) neither shows nor excludes a contribution from repetition or concentration on few formulas, and the interval's upper end allows them to account for most of BR3's loss. E18's plan reads the loss as displacement at W's size, the capacity hypothesis E6 proposed, and E16's result is consistent with it, but no experiment has tested it directly: E16 used no breadth data, and E18 did not vary size. Breadth data on W8 would test displacement directly, and is a follow-up proposal; reduced SEED exposure also has no control. Second, E13 ran in a different boot from E15 and E18: its interval is also consistent with a smaller loss, and the experiments cannot locate where the effect turns negative. E9's warm start also lost dev on the common formulas, through a takeover of unprompted output; E15 and E18 showed no takeover (title shifts +0.041, +0.005 and +0.017). That is a contrast, not a dose series, since E9 used a different recipe at a share of about 0.25. Reaching M3's 10% across 40 tasks would need both: more formulas, and each one more reliable.

7.3Limitations and threats to validity

  • Audits have one rater and 50 draws per arm. A second rating of 30 items agreed on 26 exact verdicts and 29 usable-or-not calls, but differences of a few passes between arms are within noise. Reviewers also differ: the retrained L4's passes (one draw of 50, labelled separately for E11 and E13) were 100% genuine against 82% in E10's audit (Fisher p 0.003), likely a more lenient reviewer, though models and draws differ too. Genuine shares are comparable only within one audit. E18's reviewer re-labelled E15's L4 draw and moved it from 47 to 42 genuine, as large as E18's arm gaps, partly because it counted label swaps as coincidental where E16's reviewer counted them genuine.
  • Scores come from the integer reference in the emulator. The calculator is proven bit-exact on its check programs, so this is sound for the shipped decoder, but the task suite has never run on the device, which has no prompt input yet (M4).
  • The scored decoder differs from the shipped one. Task correctness uses v2.1 decoding; v2.2 and later ship LD2. E14's attempt to score LD2 was confounded by a seeding artifact in its prompted protocol: the first stop-hazard draw is unmixed, so 27.7% of LD2 dev samples stopped at the first token, concentrated at small sampling seeds (66.6% at seeds 1 to 10). LD2's effect on correctness remains unmeasured.
  • Environment changes split comparisons. A system update on 25 October changed the training environment, and a reboot on 27 October cleared the compiled-kernel cache. E11's L4 arm trained before the reboot and its L8 arm after, with torch.compile kernels rebuilt in between; step-0 validation losses differed by up to 5.2e-7, and the effect on task scores is unmeasured and could have either sign. L4 retrained after the update scored 4.45% against E10's 4.73% (−0.28, p 0.25, descriptive), so comparisons with E10's numbers cross environments and are not controlled.
  • Breadth share is confounded with repetition. E15's copies raised the breadth share 2.8× and the passes per program from 121 to 340 together. There is no arm with equal SEED exposure, and SEED passes fell from 33.7 to 32.4. E18's BR-wide-hi holds E15's share at about 100 passes per program, but it also changes the formula count, program source and length, so E18 cannot separate repetition from concentration.
  • Seed-to-seed spread is large against the effects. The same L4 recipe has averaged 4.73% (E10), 4.45% (E11), 5.00% (E15) and 4.57% (E22, retrained in its own boot) over 16 seeds; E22’s retrained control drew below E15’s L4 (dev −0.43; held-out −0.14, CI −0.26 to −0.01). Even a same-seed retrain on the same machine is a fresh draw: E24’s retrained L4c matched E22’s on average (+0.02, CI −0.65 to +0.68), but per seed the difference ran from −1.85 to +2.35 points (SD 1.25). The paired SDs of the main contrasts, about 1.70 (E16), 1.70 (E19’s P1), 1.29 (E21’s P1) and 1.68 (E24), are larger than the margins being judged (E19 +0.87, E22 +0.66, E24 +0.92), so a margin under a point carries a wide interval even at 16 seeds. Across boots, E15's L4 minus E11's is +0.55 (CI −0.13 to +1.23), BR3 minus E13's BR −0.53 (CI −1.34 to +0.27), and BR3 minus E13's L4 control −0.54 (CI −1.29 to +0.20); dose and environment are confounded in all three.
  • The control in E16 and E18 was reused, not interleaved. E15's L4 trained a day before E16's arms and about two days before E18's, in the same boot with the same code and compile cache. The reuse checks rule out code, environment and scoring drift, but arm is confounded with the time window, and so is E18's BR-wide-hi − BR3 contrast. E16's spread was also larger than planned (paired SD about 1.70 against 1.28): W8 passed anyway, and D9's null is less informative than planned. E18's T_sparse was noisier still (paired SD about 1.5 to 2× the plan's), so its nulls are less informative than planned except where the exclusion bound fired (BR-wide).
  • Capacity was probed at one step and one shape. W8 is one size step (+32%), and the curve beyond it is unknown; D9 is one shape at one lr. E16's device figures are lab.budget estimates with int8 key/value caches, not hardware measurements.
  • E19 is a re-score with post-training 4-bit K/V. P1 re-tests E16's 16 checkpoint pairs with fresh draws under a different K/V format, so it is not a new training draw or a replication of E16. Only post-training quantization was tested there; E21 later tested one QAT fine-tune and E23 from-scratch QAT (below). Scores use v2.1 prompted decoding, so they are results with device-format K/V, not with the device's decoder (LD2). Its device figures are lab.budget estimates; E20 later measured the 4-bit unpack cost and horizon savings in CEmu (below). P2 rests on one task, the secondaries are uncorrected, and the audit is one rater with 50 passes per target.
  • E20's speed is an extrapolation, and its RAM fit is conditional. Cycles were counted in CEmu and converted to calculator speed with r = 1 (v2.2's hardware check; r = 0.994 from rc2's is the low end). CEmu matched whole programs within the two-digit readings of v2.2 and rc2, but the 4-bit unpack, a table-lookup loop, has never been timed on hardware, and placement B's tie with A is CEmu's timing model. LD2 ran with rc2's borrowed offsets, so W8's program lengths are not its own, and its LD2 cost comes from one 162-token program. User RAM fits only if the C libraries need under 22,755 B, which was not measured. The host tests and T1 were re-run and recorded on 3 November, all passing. E20 measures cost, not quality.
  • E21 tested one QAT recipe. It is a 6,000-step fine-tune of PTQ checkpoints at one lr schedule; from-scratch QAT was tested later (E23, below), and other recipes are untested. The decision record’s secondary 4-bit agreement summary is stale, judged against a superseded floor (erratum, 11 November; no reading changes). Its controls are reused (W8k4, W8i8, L4i8): W8f removes the time-window and extra-step confounds from A only, while P1 and C carry them and W8k4's selection effect. Spread was larger than planned (P1's paired SD 1.29 against 0.80 to 1.06). The fine-tuned arms' lean below their sources is exploratory: no two-sided interval excludes zero, the contrasts share controls, and contrasts against W8i8 may carry a mirror of W8k4's selection effect. Two bug-check thresholds changed after the freeze by recorded amendment (A1 and A2, the second correcting a mis-set threshold); neither touched the training code or any outcome measure.
  • E22 judged a package, partly descriptively. The cleaning is one package (source drop, scrub, dedup, span cap), so no effect is attributed to any part. No local rater passed acceptance on the author’s labels, so the credit-pattern count (P1) is descriptive and sees only what the scrubber’s own patterns match; the name audit used a weak local model. The retrained L4 control drew below E15’s L4, so P3’s positive point estimate is partly a low control.
  • E23 and E24 are single draws with wide intervals. E23’s fit gain has a CI from 0.5% to 86% of E19’s cost, and its task result excludes a gain of half E19’s cost but not a loss of 0.5 points. E24’s pass is marginal (lower bound +0.02) and both its one-point readings are open: 16 seeds at its observed SD give about ±0.9 points, and the plan’s estimate for 80% power at E19’s margin was about 21 seeds; seeds are not extended after an outcome. E23’s QAT used E21’s untuned scale tracking. Neither experiment ran a model on the calculator.
  • E23 and E24 ran under standing direction and on one host. Both plans were approved under the author’s standing direction, and several run-handling decisions (hand validations, resumes, a moved host) were not individually approved by the author; each is recorded. Both runs had an unnoticed stop (about 10 h and 5 h). E24 was planned for the Sparks and ran wholly on the PC’s 5090 under amendment A4, so its per-host comparison is degenerate. E24’s 895 tests pass only with CUDA hidden; with the 5090 visible, 16 export tests trip a guard (a test-environment issue; the E24 write-up, note of 11 November).
  • Later audits use an unvalidated local rater. E22 to E24’s blind audits were labelled by one local open-weight model, weaker than the reviewers of E6 to E21 and not validated against human labels for this task: on E22’s acceptance test it found 10 of the 17 spans the author had marked. Its rubric prompt and the labelling scripts were written after each outcome was known. E24’s sample was drawn after the result, with the arm order, which changes which items are drawn, chosen by the labelling script’s writer (comparator first, as in E16 to E23). At 50 programs per arm the audits are underpowered for small differences, and their genuine shares (90 to 98%) are not comparable with earlier audits’.
  • The menu sweep is exploratory. Its rules were stated before any program was generated, but no test decides anything and nothing is corrected for multiplicity. W8c4’s programs come from the Python reference with 4-bit K/V, since no 4-bit device build offers topics; the LD2 fits cover positions 0 to 255 only; the SEEDc checkpoints were fitted on SEED’s training programs; and the paired intervals treat the seed number as the pair (Section 5.25).
  • The primary metric still credits later numbers. M3 stays on suite v2, where the reference can pass with other numbers shown after it. Suite v3's stricter rules remove 12 to 18% of dev v2 passes in the re-scored arms; no verdict changed, but two descriptive contrasts shrank (Section 3.6).
  • Recent nulls are wide. E11's interval runs from −0.51 to +1.05 points, E13's dev interval from −0.54 to +0.52, E16's D9 interval from −1.20 to +0.60 and E18's BR-wide dev interval from −1.05 to +0.34, so none excludes effects of about half a point; BR-wide's upper bound does exclude +0.8.
  • Leak screening is strong but not perfect. E13's and E15's breadth data carried variants of flagged specifications (2.4 to 4.75% of programs), found by E18's review after both decisions; neither verdict changed (Sections 5.14, 5.16). E18 used two blind reviewers per sheet and dropped the variants of every flagged specification, and reviewer blindness is by instruction only.
  • Held-out counts are tiny. 78 passes in 32,000 samples was E10's best result, and E16's W8 had 114 pooled passes, so held-out effects are reported as secondary or descriptive.
  • Task selection. The suite leans toward standard school formulas, which overlap with the synthetic data's topic taxonomy and with common human programs.
  • Selection optimism. v2.3-rc2's 6.45% is the best of 16 seeds; the arm mean, 4.73%, is the honest estimate.
  • Reused comparators and recipe-against-recipe tests. E7 compared each shape at a different lr; E8 compared equal steps at unequal compute; E9 changed its recipe by amendment before the freeze. Each is stated in its write-up.
  • v2.3-rc1 has not run on hardware. Its speed is a CEmu figure. CEmu matched the calculator for v2.1 (0.903 predicted, 0.90 measured), v2.2 (0.820 vs 0.82) and v2.3-rc2 (0.674 vs 0.67).
  • Licensing. The weights are trained on other people's programs and cannot be released before a per-source license review.

9Glossary

Ternary, 1.58-bit
Each weight is −1, 0 or +1. Three values carry log23 ≈ 1.58 bits. Multiplication becomes add, subtract or skip.
QAT
Quantization-aware training. The network trains with its weights rounded to ternary in the forward pass, so it learns to work with them. E21's 4-bit K/V QAT fine-tune also simulated the 4-bit K/V rounding in training (a fake quant matching model/kv4.py), starting from E16's trained W8 checkpoints; E23 simulated it from the first training step, from scratch.
W8q, W8f, W8r, W8r4
E21's arms: W8q, E16's W8 fine-tuned 6,000 steps with 4-bit K/V QAT, scored at 4-bit (W8q8 at int8); W8f, the same fine-tune without the fake quant (W8f8 at int8), the attribution control. E23 reused the name W8q for W8 trained from scratch with 4-bit K/V QAT; its control W8r is W8 trained again at int8 beside it, and W8r4 is W8r with post-training 4-bit K/V.
A, C, G (E21)
Readings beside E21's rule: A = W8q − W8f at 4-bit (is a gain QAT-specific?); C = QAT's own 4-bit cost minus PTQ's; G = W8q8 − W8i8 (the QAT model at int8 against E16's W8).
PTQ
Post-training quantization: rounding a trained model's values to a coarser format without retraining. E19 applied it to the K/V cache.
4-bit K/V (k4, i8)
E19's key/value cache format (model/kv4.py): 15 levels per channel, calibrated per checkpoint, with a fixed-point encode the calculator must match bit for bit. k4 marks a target with 4-bit K/V, i8 one with int8. More generally, a model name ending in k4 or 4 (W8k4, W8r4, W8c4) is scored with 4-bit K/V, and one ending in i8 or 8 (L4i8, W8q8, W8c8) with int8.
Decode-to-scratch, CLMKV4
E20's 4-bit design: K and V are stored as 4-bit codes and unpacked once per token into an int8 scratch window, so the shipped attention routines run unchanged. CLMKV4 is the AppVar that holds the 4-bit tables, read in place from archive (placement A) or copied to RAM (placement B).
r (CEmu to calculator)
The ratio of measured calculator speed to CEmu's prediction on a hardware check: 1 from v2.2's, 0.994 from rc2's. E20 extrapolates tok/s with r = 1.
O1, O2, O3
The author's open device decisions from the E19 plan: O1 the cycle budget, O2 whether to pursue a W8-sized model, O3 the AppVar cap, file limit and archive space.
intref
The integer reference implementation in Python. The calculator must match it bit for bit.
AppVar
A calculator data file, at most 65,505 bytes, stored in archive flash. The model is split across 11 (S) or 16 (W) of them.
CEmu
The TI-84 Plus CE emulator, run headless with the author's own ROM to judge programs and count cycles.
ALiBi, horizons
Positions encoded as a per-head penalty on distance. Horizons skip keys whose penalty has saturated.
LD2
The linker decoder with a full-vocabulary stop hazard: enforces label linkage and plausible endings while sampling.
strict OK
An unprompted program ends normally and shows output beyond echoing its inputs.
task_correct
Share of task-suite completions whose screens show the reference answer for all three input tuples and nothing wrong. dev: the 40 development tasks.
Held-out
The 20 suite-v2 tasks never used in training data, prompts or rewards; a test of new formulas.
Genuine, partial, coincidental
Blind-audit labels: computes the task's formula; right answer with a visible defect; right number for the wrong reason.
k
Completions sampled per task per checkpoint (25 or 50 for dev, 100 for held-out).
Paired t-test
Compares two arms using per-seed differences, since both arms share a seed's training batches or base model. One-sided: only "better" counts.
Holm
A correction for two or more tests: the smallest p must beat 0.05 divided by the number of tests, the next 0.05 divided by one fewer, and so on.
Guard
A preregistered side condition, such as validation loss or copying, that an arm must not break to be adopted.
Freeze
A hashed snapshot of code, data and plan; the runner refuses to decide if anything changes.
Development seed
Seed 10, run before the freeze to check the pipeline and make rule-based choices. It never enters a decision.
Row N
A row of an experiment's preregistered decision table. The row the results fall in names the next step: E21's row 6 weakened the device case for W8, E22's row 7 left the data decision to the author, E23's row 9 kept PTQ and E24's row 8 left W8c4's adoption to the author.
P1, P2, P3, S1; reading
P marks a primary question, which decides (with Holm where there are several); S marks a secondary, tested only under a stated condition or reported descriptively (E23's S1 was tested only if P1 passed; E24's S1 is descriptive). A reading is an interpretation fixed in advance and stated beside the rule; it decides nothing. Numbers restart in each plan.
U1, U2, O, BR (E24)
E24's readings: U1 and U2, whether a margin of at least one point is shown (U1) or excluded (U2) by the one-sided bounds; O, whether the pass survives dropping its best task; BR, “broad”, held-out and v3 dev point estimates both positive, whether or not either is significant.
SEEDc, L4c
SEEDc is SEED rebuilt from the cleaned corpus (E22): Axe and ICE sources dropped, then the scrub, deduplication and the span cap; 36,368 human programs and 4,598 seed copies. L4c is the L4 recipe (W, 60,000 steps) trained on SEEDc. The author adopted SEEDc on 11 November.
W8c, W8c4, W8c8
E24's W8 trained on SEEDc. W8c4 is W8c with post-training 4-bit K/V (tables calibrated on SEEDc); W8c8 is the same model at int8.
Non-inferiority, margin
A one-sided test that an arm is not worse than its control by more than a margin fixed in advance; it passes when the arm's lower bound is above minus the margin. E22's margin was 0.75 points, and its p is against the margin.
NLL, nats, fit
Negative log-likelihood of held-out text per token, in nats (natural-log units); lower is a better fit. E21 and E23 used it as the fit measure.
Real-content copying, informative window
E22's P2: the share of unprompted programs at least half of whose informative 32-token windows also occur in the training data, counting only programs with at least 8 such windows. An informative window holds at least 5 distinct token ids, so filler does not count.
Scrubber, credit-pattern count
E22's scrubber deletes text inside strings where fixed rules match: credit and byline clauses, e-mail and web addresses, @handles and a list of names. The credit-pattern count (E22's P1) is the share of programs with a match of the credit and address rules. It sees only what those rules match, so it cannot show that a model is free of credit lines.
Deduplication, span cap
E22's deduplication drops exact copies, near-duplicate programs and programs mostly contained in a longer one. The span cap keeps any informative 32-token window in at most 4 programs and cuts it, by whole lines, from the rest; a program that would lose more than half its tokens is dropped.
Axe, ICE
Two compilers for the calculator whose source files are stored like TI-BASIC programs but are not TI-BASIC. E22 dropped their sources from the training data.
Mask level
Which token masks the decoder applies. Level 1 is v2.3's masks and the default in every experiment; level 2 adds v2.4's safety masks (no credit line can start inside a string, no calls to the user's programs or the app libraries, no settings changes that outlive the program). E22's plan also names a level 0 for the release review's rerun, which the current code does not offer.
Amendment, deviation; their labels
An amendment is a recorded change to an approved or frozen plan, with what had been seen at the time; a deviation records where a run departed from its plan, for example under one of the plan's fallbacks. Labels are per experiment: E21's A1 and A2; E22's R1 (a deviation) and R2 to R4; E24's A3 and A4 (hosts) and R-1 to R-3 (runner bug fixes). E22's scrubber rules are also numbered R1 to R5; the two series are unrelated.
Control N
A numbered check in a plan, run before or at the decision, such as reproducing a reused record byte for byte or checking that a fake quant was active. A failed control stops the run until it is fixed or a recorded amendment covers it.
Development reading
A reading computed on the development seed before the freeze, as a check of the pipeline; it never enters a decision. E24's was not produced.
Phases 1 to 6
The roadmap of 3 November, dated to the author: 1, v2.4's safety masks; 2, preparing a public release; 3, v2.5's topic menu; 4, E22; 5, E23; 6, a local browser demo of the device core in WebAssembly.
Topic menu, menu hit rate
v2.5-rc1's on-device menu: the calculator types a task opening for one of ten formulas and the model finishes it, each entry showing its measured hit rate. The menu hit rate is the share of programs right across the ten topics at equal k, with LD2, a 256-token cap and mask level 2 (Section 5.25).
Release review
A local review of what a release candidate writes before it ships: unprompted programs checked for running, credit lines, copying and risky commands, and prompted correctness on the task suite (Section 6).
GB10
The NVIDIA chip in a DGX Spark. E24's plan named it as the training platform; E24 trained on the PC's RTX 5090 instead.
BSS, CLMTMP, ARC FREE
BSS is the calculator program's fixed RAM region (60,690 bytes), holding the key cache, the tokens and the core state; CLMTMP is a temporary variable in user RAM (at most 65,505 bytes) holding the value cache and the LD2 workspace; ARC FREE is the free archive space the calculator reports, which E20 did not read.
Local rater
A local open-weight language model used from E22 on to label blind audits and names in programs. It failed acceptance against the author's labels in E22 and was not validated for audit labelling.
Standing direction
The author's standing instructions to keep work moving without stopping for each approval. Decisions taken under it are recorded as not individually approved by the author.

DPlain-language glossary

One line per term, for readers without an AI or statistics background. Section 9 has the technical definitions.

Token
One word-sized piece of a program, such as Prompt, + or a digit. The model writes one at a time.
Weights
The model's internal numbers, set by training. CalcLM's main weights are each −1, 0 or +1 (ternary).
Layer
One stage of the model's processing. W has 6 stacked layers; W8 has 8.
Step
One small training update. The original recipe trained for 15,000 steps.
Seed
The random number that sets a training run's starting point and reading order. Different seeds give different copies of the same recipe.
Decision seeds, seed 10
Seeds 11 to 26 decide every experiment since E10. Seed 10 is a rehearsal and never counts.
Recipe, arm, control
A recipe is one way of training; each recipe tested in an experiment is an arm; the control is the arm the others are compared with, usually L4.
S
The original small model shape (2.44M weights), used in releases v2 to v2.2.
W
The wide shape (3.74M weights), the largest that comfortably fits the calculator; the base model since E7b.
L2, L4, L8
W trained 2, 4 or 8 times the original 15,000 steps. L4 (60,000 steps) is the current recipe.
W8
W with 8 layers instead of 6, about a third more weights. Best scores so far (6.60% in E16; 6.88% on the cleaned data with 8-bit notes in E24), but it does not fit the calculator: even with 4-bit notes it is slower than allowed and needs more files and storage (E20).
D9
A deeper, narrower shape (9 layers, width 192) of about W's size. Tested in E16; no gain detected.
Corpus
About 41,000 TI-BASIC programs people published online, used to train the model. Kept private.
SEED
The standard training data since E7: the corpus plus 2,413 real math programs from it, repeated so they make up more of it.
SYN
3,000 math programs written by another AI and checked in the emulator (E4, E6).
GPT-6.1-Sol
The outside AI model that wrote the synthetic and breadth programs.
Breadth data
Extra example programs covering many formulas, each checked in the emulator, meant to teach the model more formulas.
Spec
A short description of one formula task, from which programs are written. Reviewers drop specs too close to a test task.
BR
E13's breadth set: 2,104 programs for 217 formulas, shown once, about 1.4% of training text.
BR3
E15's arm: three copies of BR, about 3.8% of training text.
BR-wide
E18's arm with programs for 956 formulas at E13's share (about 1.4%).
BR-wide-hi
E18's arm with the same 956 formulas at E15's share (about 4.0%), each program shown once.
Share (E), dose
The fraction of all training text that is breadth programs.
Passes per program
How many times training reads each breadth program (about 121, 340 and 100 in E13, E15 and BR-wide-hi).
Task suite
The set of specific requests, each with a checked answer, that defines "correct".
task_correct
The share of attempts whose screen shows the right answer for all three sets of inputs, with nothing wrong.
dev
The 40 main tasks. M3 is measured on them.
Held-out
20 further tasks whose formulas are never used for training: a test of new formulas.
Taught, untaught
Practice tasks whose formulas are in the breadth data (taught) or deliberately left out of it (untaught).
T_sparse
Per run, how many of 71 untaught tasks that the control almost never solves get solved at least once.
A, T, D
The breadth experiments' questions: did it absorb the taught formulas (A), transfer to untaught ones (T), and change the dev score (D)?
Transfer
Getting better at formulas the model was never shown.
Suite v1, v2, v3
Versions of the checker, each closing loopholes. M3 uses v2; v3 is a stricter score reported alongside.
Labelled score
A stricter score that also needs a fitting label word next to the answer.
Math tier
An older score that passed any program that took input and computed something; E4 showed it rewarded form, not correctness.
strict OK
An unprompted program ends normally and shows something beyond its inputs. It measures clean running, not correctness.
val loss
How well the model predicts real programs it did not train on; lower is better. It has often moved opposite to correctness.
K/V cache
The model's running notes about every token already written, which it looks back at before each new token. It lives in the calculator's memory.
int8
Storing each number in 8 bits (256 possible values); today's format for the K/V cache.
4-bit
Storing each number in 4 bits (here 15 possible values): half the space of int8, and coarser. E19 tested it for the K/V cache: W8 kept its lead, at a small cost to both models. E20 measured what it costs in speed on the emulated calculator.
PTQ
Rounding a finished model's numbers to a coarser format without retraining it. E19 tested 4-bit notes this way.
QAT
Training with the coarse format built in, so the model learns to work with it. CalcLM's weights are always trained this way. E21 tried a short round of it with 4-bit notes on the finished W8 models, and it did not help; E23 trained with 4-bit notes from the start, which improved the fit but not the score.
Fine-tune
A short round of extra training that starts from an already trained model.
W8q, W8f, W8r
E21's versions of W8: W8q trained a little longer with 4-bit notes built in; W8f got the same extra training without them, to tell the two effects apart. In E23, W8q is W8 trained with 4-bit notes from the start, and W8r ordinary W8 trained again beside it (W8r4 with 4-bit notes added after training).
Spike, test build
A small, quick build to measure something before committing to the full work. E20 was one: W8 with 4-bit notes in CEmu, to measure its real speed and memory.
Extrapolated
Worked out from the emulator's count of the calculator's work rather than timed on a real calculator. E20's speed is extrapolated this way; the emulator matched the calculator on two earlier checks.
O1, O2, O3
The author's open budget calls for a bigger model: the speed limit, whether to pursue a W8-sized model, and the limits on file count, file size and storage space.
RAM, archive
The calculator's small working memory, and its larger flash storage where the model's files live.
AppVar
A calculator data file of at most 65,505 bytes. The model is split across them, and the program allows at most 16.
Cycles, tok/s
The calculator's work per token, and the resulting speed in tokens per second. The budget allows 1.5× v2.1's work per token.
lab.budget
The project's tool that estimates whether a model fits the calculator's memory and file limits.
CEmu
An emulator: a faithful software copy of the calculator, used to run and judge programs on a PC.
intref
The reference version of the model, in whole-number arithmetic, that the calculator must match exactly.
Hardware check
A release counts only after the real calculator writes the same program, byte for byte, as intref with the same seed.
Decoder, LD2, V21
The procedure that turns the model's predictions into a program. LD2, the linker decoder, ships since v2.2; V21, the older v2.1 decoding, is what the task suite scores.
Tracker, masks, horizons
Helpers in the decoder: the tracker follows the program as it is written, masks block words certain to cause an error, and horizons skip far-back words to save time.
Judge pool
Emulator judging split across the PC and the author's two DGX Sparks, giving identical results faster.
Sparks
The author's two DGX Spark computers, used to judge programs, not to train models.
Preregistration
Writing down the question, rules and pass marks before any result exists.
Freeze
A stored fingerprint of code, plan and data; the software refuses to decide if anything changes.
Amendment
A change after the plan is fixed, recorded openly with what had been seen at the time.
Primary, secondary
Primary scores decide the verdict; secondary scores are reported but decide nothing.
Guard
A tripwire that fails the experiment if something else got worse.
Paired test
Comparing two recipes by the differences between runs that share a seed.
p value
How often luck alone would produce a result at least this favourable if the change really did nothing.
95% confidence interval
The range of true effect sizes that fit the data.
Not detected
The data cannot tell the effect apart from zero. It is not the same as "does not work".
Ruled out, excluded
The data's upper bound is below a size fixed in advance (usually +0.8), so an effect that large is very unlikely.
Holm
A stricter pass mark when several main questions are tested at once, so luck gets no extra chances.
Blind audit
A fresh reviewer model reads 50 passing programs per arm without knowing the arm.
Genuine, partial, coincidental
Audit labels: computes the right formula; right answer with a visible flaw; right number for the wrong reason.
Boot, reboot
Restarting the training PC rebuilds some compiled code, so runs from different boots are not perfectly comparable.
Reused control
In E16 and E18, E15's L4 runs were reused instead of retrained, after checks showed nothing had changed.
Distillation
Training a model to copy a larger teacher model's guesses (E8).
Warm start
Extra training on practice tasks before anything else (E9).
Expert iteration
Retraining a model on its own answers that the emulator confirmed correct (planned in E9, not tested).
M0 to M5, M3
The roadmap's milestones. M3, the current one, is 10% task_correct with a recipe that could ship.
v2.2, v2.3-rc2, v2.6
The release with the original small model (18 October); the first wide-model candidate, which passed its hardware check on 28 October and was not released; and the current calculator release (21 November), the hardware-checked v2.6-rc2 bundle unchanged.
Row N
One line of an experiment's decision table, fixed in advance; the line the results land on says what happens next.
P1, P2, S1, reading
P questions decide an experiment; S questions are side questions; a reading is an interpretation stated in advance that decides nothing.
U1, U2, O, BR
E24's readings: whether a lead of a full point is shown (U1) or ruled out (U2), whether the lead survives dropping its best task (O), and whether the held-out and stricter scores lean the same way (BR, “broad”).
SEEDc, L4c
The cleaned training data from E22, and today's recipe trained on it. SEEDc was adopted on 11 November.
W8c, W8c4, W8c8
W8 trained on the cleaned data (E24), scored with 4-bit notes (W8c4) or 8-bit notes (W8c8).
Name endings k4, 4, i8, 8
A model name ending in k4 or 4 means it was scored with 4-bit notes; i8 or 8 means 8-bit notes.
Non-inferior, margin
Shown to be no worse than the comparison by more than an amount fixed in advance (the margin).
NLL, nats, fit
How well the model predicts text it did not train on, in units called nats; lower is better.
Real-content copying
How often a program repeats a stretch of training text that carries real content rather than filler (informative windows).
Scrubber, credit-pattern count
E22's tool that deletes credit, address and name text inside strings, and the count of programs where its patterns still match; the count only sees what the patterns match.
Dedup, span cap
Removing duplicate programs, and cutting any block of code shared by more than 4 programs out of the rest.
Axe, ICE
Two other calculator languages whose sources were in the corpus; E22 dropped them.
Mask levels
Level 1: the decoder blocks words certain to cause an error. Level 2 also blocks credit lines in text, calls to other programs and lasting settings changes.
Amendment and deviation labels
Short names such as A1 or R3 for recorded changes to a plan, numbered within each experiment.
Control N
A numbered check in a plan that must pass before results count.
Development reading
A reading taken on the rehearsal seed; it never counts.
Phases 1 to 6
The work plan of 3 November: safety masks, release preparation, the topic menu, E22, E23 and a browser demo.
Topic menu, menu hit rate
The calculator's list of ten formulas the model can write, and the share of menu programs it gets right.
Release review
A check of what a candidate writes before it ships: whether its programs run, copy training text or carry names.
GB10
The chip in a DGX Spark.
BSS, CLMTMP, ARC FREE
Two of the calculator's memory areas the model uses, and its free storage space.
Local rater
A language model run on the project's PC to label programs in later audits; it was never shown to agree with human labels.
Standing direction
The author's standing instructions to keep work moving; decisions taken under them were not individually approved.
